# Pendulum Clock: guide's notes

These notes are for the guide only. The module is one 2-hour session for up to 16 students in teams of three (one times, one counts, one records; swap roles between tests). It runs indoors and needs no electricity.

The module uses a **seconds pendulum**: it **ticks** (swings from one side to the other) once a second, so its **period** (one full swing, there and back) is **2 s**, and its length is about **99 cm**. We chose this over a 25 cm pendulum with a 1 s period because the longer pendulum swings slowly enough to count without mistakes, 10 full swings take a comfortable 20 s, and it is the real length used in tall clocks. Keep the words "tick", "full swing" and "period" consistent all session; most wrong answers come from mixing them up.

---

## Before the session

### 1. Hanging points (the day before)

Each team needs a stick fixed at least 1.3 m above the floor (a 1 m pendulum plus room for the nut and the clip). Good options:

- a broom handle tied firmly across the window burglar bars,
- a broom handle across a doorway, resting on two nails or tied to the frame,
- a stick between two tall shelves or cupboards, weighted or tied down.

Tie them yourself. Students must not climb on furniture. Leave about 1 m of space either side of each pendulum so teams don't hit each other.

### 2. Shopping

- Large steel nuts (M16–M20), 3 per team plus spares. Weigh one on a kitchen scale if you can; 30–60 g is ideal. Heavier is better: it keeps swinging longer.
- Mason's line from a hardware shop. Cut 1.5 m per team, plus spares. Cotton thread is fine; avoid stretchy nylon fishing line and wool.
- Large binder clips, 2 per team.
- Masking tape, markers, graph paper (2 sheets per team), protractors, metre sticks.
- Install a free metronome app on your phone and set it to 60 beats per minute.

### 3. Build the hook pendulum

Hang a padlock on mason's line from a binder clip so that the distance from the clip's jaws to the middle of the padlock is **99 cm**. Check it: 10 full swings should take 19.8–20.2 s. Adjust until they do. Mark the string at the clip. Do the same with a second, heavier padlock at the same length. Finally, put a tape mark on one string at 25 cm from the padlock's middle, so you can quickly shorten it during the hook.

---

## Safety briefing (read aloud at the start)

- Stand beside a swinging pendulum, never in its path. Keep your face away from the nut.
- Nobody climbs on chairs or desks. If a hanging point needs changing, call the guide.
- Pull the nut back gently and let it go. Don't throw it or swing it round in circles.

The risks here are small; the main one is a nut swung hard into someone's face.

## Session plan

### Step 1: The ticking padlock (15 min)

Start the metronome. Pull the padlock about 10 cm to one side and release it on a click. It passes the middle on every click. Point out: one click = one tick; there and back = two clicks = one period of 2 s.

Then swap in the heavier padlock at the same length: it keeps the same beat. Then shorten the string to the 25 cm mark (hold the string at the mark against the clip): now it does a whole there-and-back for each click. A quarter of the length gives half the period. Don't explain any of this. Let students write their predictions and questions.

**Common predictions:** most students predict heavier = faster and bigger swing = slower. Good: the fair tests will surprise them. Collect their length guesses for a 1-second tick; they range wildly (10 cm to 3 m).

### Step 2: What sets the beat? (35 min)

Check every team's length measurement before they start: **clip jaws to the middle of the nut**. Measuring to the top of the nut, or from the stick rather than the clip, is the most common setup error.

Watch the first timing of each team. Two mistakes to catch:

- counting "one" when they start the stopwatch, so they time 9 swings, not 10 (their period comes out about 10% short),
- counting every pass of the middle as a swing, so they time 5 full swings (their period comes out half the right value).

**Expected results** (Addis Ababa, small swings; numbers to within about ±0.2 s for 10 swings):

| Test | Setting | 10 full swings | Period |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: mass | 1 nut, 50 cm | 14.2 s | 1.42 s |
| A: mass | 3 nuts, 50 cm | 14.2 s | 1.42 s |
| B: swing size | 5 cm pull, 50 cm | 14.2 s | 1.42 s |
| B: swing size | 20 cm pull, 50 cm | about 14.4 s | about 1.44 s |
| C: length | 25 cm | 10.0 s | 1.00 s |
| C: length | 100 cm | 20.1 s | 2.01 s |

The 20 cm pull on a 50 cm string is a swing of about 24°, which makes the period about 1% longer. That is 0.15 s on 10 swings, smaller than the timing error, so students should conclude "no measurable difference". If a strong team insists the big swing is slightly slower, they're right: praise it, and say the rule is only exact for small swings.

**Where students get stuck:** in test A, taping three nuts in a line below the first makes the pendulum longer. The middle of the bunch must still be at 50 cm. If a team finds the heavy pendulum "slower", check this first.

### Step 3: Tune it (45 min)

**Expected results:**

| L (m) | 10 swings (s) | T (s) | T² (s²) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25 | 10.0 | 1.00 | 1.01 |
| 0.40 | 12.7 | 1.27 | 1.62 |
| 0.60 | 15.6 | 1.56 | 2.42 |
| 0.80 | 18.0 | 1.80 | 3.23 |
| 1.00 | 20.1 | 2.01 | 4.04 |

The T² graph should be a straight line through the origin with a gradient of about **4.0 s² per metre** (in theory 4π²/g = 4.04 s²/m for Addis Ababa). Reading across from T² = 4 gives **L ≈ 0.99 m**. Accept 0.95–1.03 m as a prediction.

Tuning: a 1 cm change in length changes the 10-swing time by about 0.1 s. Teams within 19.8–20.2 s are within about ±2 cm of the ideal length, which is fine.

**Where students get stuck:**

- Squaring: some square the 10-swing time instead of the period. Check one row of every table.
- Scales: T² axis 0–4.5 s² and L axis 0–1.0 m fit nicely. Insist both axes start at zero, so students can see the line goes through the origin.
- Joining the dots on the T-against-L graph. Ask for one smooth curve.
- The gradient: pick two points on the line, far apart, for example (0, 0) and (1.0, 4.0): gradient = 4.0 ÷ 1.0 = 4.0 s²/m.

If time is short, let teams skip the 40 cm length.

### Step 4: The one-minute test (25 min)

60 ticks = 30 full swings = 60 s for a perfect seconds pendulum. Within ±1 s means the length is within about ±3 cm. A well-tuned pendulum often hits 59.6–60.4 s. The swing dies away a little over the minute; that doesn't matter, because the period doesn't depend on swing size.

**Answer key for the predictions:**

1. Period 4 s: T² = 16 s². L = 16 ÷ 4.04 ≈ **4.0 m**. Or with the pattern: twice the period needs four times the length, 4 × 1 m = 4 m. (Too tall for the classroom: that's why tall clocks use 1 m.)
2. Period 1 s: T² = 1 s². L = 1 ÷ 4.04 ≈ **0.25 m** (25 cm). Or: half the period needs a quarter of the length.
3. A clock running slow has a period that's too long, so its pendulum is too long: **shorten it** (real clocks have a nut under the bob to raise it).

Do the sign-off conversations during the one-minute test, team by team.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause and fix |
|---|---|
| Periods jump around between repeats | Counting mistakes (9 or 11 swings), or the nut is twisting or swinging in an oval. Release it gently from rest, don't push. |
| Pendulum stops swinging quickly | Nut too light, or the string is rubbing on something. Use a heavier nut and check the string hangs freely. |
| Period slowly changes during a test | The string is slipping in the clip. Use a bigger clip, or wrap a bit of tape round the string where it is clipped. |
| T² line doesn't go through the origin | Lengths measured to the wrong point (top of nut, or from the stick). A consistent error of a few cm shifts the line sideways. |
| Every period is about 10% too short | They're counting "one" at the start and timing only 9 swings. |
| Every period is half the expected value | They're counting ticks (passes of the middle) instead of full swings. |

---

## Judging the sign-off items

- **fair-test:** the table shows one variable changed in each test, the length kept at 50 cm for tests A and B (ask how they kept it the same with three nuts), and at least two timings per setting.
- **period:** the student says the reaction-time error (about 0.2 s) is shared over 10 swings. Ask them to work out the period from, say, 17.0 s for 10 swings (1.70 s).
- **graph:** both axes labelled with units, T² on the y-axis, a single straight line (not dot-to-dot), and a reading taken from the line at T² = 16 or by extending it. "About 4 m" is the target.
- **ticks-true:** watch both timings yourself. 59–61 s passes. Check they are counting ticks (60) and not full swings.
- **explains:** must say that mass doesn't matter, swing size doesn't matter for small swings, and length does. For the slow clock: shorten the pendulum.

## Notes for experts reviewing this draft

- The period formula T = 2π√(L/g) is deliberately not given to students; they find the T² ∝ L pattern from their own graph. Strong students can be shown it as an extension.
- Expected values use g ≈ 9.78 m/s² for Addis Ababa (about 2,350 m above sea level, latitude 9° N). This gives L = 99.1 cm for a 2 s period, against 99.4 cm with g = 9.81. The difference is far below what students can measure.
- The nut is treated as a point mass at its centre; the string's mass and the nut's size add errors of a few millimetres at most.
- The large-swing correction (about +1% at 24°, +1.7% at 30°) is mentioned in the step 2 notes only. Check this level of detail is right for a beginner module.
- Curriculum links (grade 9 measurement, grade 11 oscillations) need confirming against the current national curriculum.
