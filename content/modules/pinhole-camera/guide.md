# Pinhole Camera: guide's notes

These notes are for the guide only. The module is one 2-hour session for up to 16 students in teams of two. You need a room you can black out (prepared beforehand), a darkish corner indoors, and a sunny or bright day for steps 3 and 4. On a very overcast day the images are dim but still visible; look at the brightest scene you can find.

---

## Before the session

### 1. Shopping

| Item | How many | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| A3 thin card | 2 per team, plus spares | Dark card is best, because it lets less light through. If you only have light card, students can add a second layer or colour the inside with a black marker. |
| Aluminium foil | One small roll | |
| Tracing paper or baking paper | 1 sheet per team | |
| Pins, a ~3 mm nail, pencils | 1 of each per team | |
| Masking tape, black electrical tape, scissors, rulers | | |
| Dark cloths for hoods | 1 per team | A dark netela folded double, a black shawl or a jacket. |
| Index cards, torches, cut-out F shapes | 3 cards, 1 torch, 1 F per team | Phone torches are fine. Cut the Fs from card in advance, about 3 cm tall. |
| 30 m tape measure | 1 | |
| Black plastic sheeting and tape | Enough to cover the windows of one room | Hardware shops in Merkato. |

### 2. Prepare the dark room (the day before, or early on the day)

Choose a room with a window that looks out on a **sunlit** scene: a street, trees, a building. A window facing away from the Sun is best, so no sunlight shines straight in through the hole.

1. Cover every window and gap with black plastic or cardboard and tape. Stuff cloth under the door. The room must be **really** dark: a little stray light washes the picture out completely.
2. In the cover of the best window, cut a hole about 1–1.5 cm across, at about head height. Tape a small flap of card over it so you can cover and uncover it.
3. Hang a white sheet or tape white paper on the wall opposite the hole, if the wall isn't already pale.
4. Test it: go in, wait three minutes for your eyes to adjust, and uncover the hole. You should see the outside, upside down, across the far wall.

**No room you can black out?** Use a large cardboard carton (from a fridge or TV shop). You cut a 1 cm hole in one end and a head-sized opening in the side near the other end; tape white paper inside on the far end. Students take turns putting their heads in, with a cloth over the gap.

### 3. Other preparation

- Cut the letter Fs and pre-punch the three index cards per team (push a pencil through a stack so the holes line up).
- Cut 4 cm foil squares, at least 4 per team.
- Make your own camera following step 3, so you know the image you're aiming for.
- Walk the compound and choose two or three tall objects for step 4 (a tree, the flagpole, a building), each with at least 20–30 m of open ground in front. Measure one yourself with your camera, so you have a reference answer.

---

## Safety briefing (read aloud in step 1)

- **Never point a pinhole camera at the Sun, and never look at the Sun.** The eye has no pain sensors in the retina, so damage can happen without any warning. If the Sun is in the scene, turn away. Make sure the dark room's hole doesn't face the Sun.
- Pins in their box or stuck in a card. Collect them at the end.
- Students use scissors only. You do any blade work.
- Hoods: stand still while looking. Nobody walks with a hood on, and nobody uses one near steps, a road, or where vehicles move on the compound.

## Session plan

### Step 1: A picture from a hole (15 min)

Read the rules outside the room (4 minutes). Then take everyone in, close the door, keep the hole covered for two to three minutes while eyes adjust, then uncover it. Give them about 8 minutes inside. Ask a helper (or a student) to walk past outside the window and wave. Partly cover the hole with your hand, then uncover it: the picture gets dimmer but stays whole. If you can safely make the hole bigger with scissors for a moment, the picture becomes brighter and blurrier. Tape it back to the smaller size afterwards.

Don't explain. Collect predictions and questions.

Expected numbers, if asked: a building 10 m tall, 30 m outside the window, with the far wall 5 m from the hole, gives an image $10 \times 5 \div 30 \approx 1.7$ m tall.

**"Is left and right swapped too?"** When you face the wall (with your back to the hole), things that are on your right when you look out of the window appear on your right on the wall. The picture is flipped top to bottom only, like a reflection in a puddle. Writing on a sign looks like its reflection in water. In the hand-held camera in step 3, you look **towards** the scene through the screen, and the picture is turned half a turn: upside down **and** left–right swapped. Turn a sketch of it round and it reads normally. Both follow from the same rule: each ray goes straight through the hole.

### Step 2: Light in straight lines (35 min)

**Three cards (10 min).** Expected: light gets through all three only when the holes are in line. Slide the middle card 1 cm and the spot on the paper disappears. The tight string passes through all three holes only when they are lined up, so the light's path and the tight string follow the same straight line.

**Upside-down F (10 min).** It needs real darkness: a corner with a jacket hung over chairs works. With the torch 20 cm in front of the hole and the paper 20 cm behind, the F on the paper is the same size as the cut-out F (about 3 cm) and upside down. Looking at the front of the paper, it looks like the F's reflection in a puddle: flipped top to bottom. At 40 cm it is about 6 cm tall and noticeably dimmer, because the same light is spread over four times the area.

**Theory (7 min).** *Light and images* is short; most students finish it in the time.

**Ray diagrams (12 min).** Students often draw rays that bend at the hole. Insist on a ruler and one straight line from each point of the object, through the dot, to the screen. Expected: at 5 cm (20 cm real), the image is 3 cm tall; at 10 cm (40 cm real), 6 cm. Both upside down. Some students ask why they can draw distances smaller but heights full size: stretching or squashing a drawing in one direction keeps straight lines straight, so where the rays land on the screen line doesn't change in proportion. If that worries them, they can draw it fully to scale on a large sheet.

Model answer for the record: *the ray from the top of the F goes down through the hole and lands at the bottom of the screen, and the ray from the bottom goes up, so they cross at the hole and the image is upside down. Moving the screen back lets the rays spread further apart before they land, so the image is bigger.*

### Step 3: Build the camera (45 min)

**Building (20 min).** Common faults:

- The inner tube is too tight or too loose. Too tight: unroll and re-tape slightly bigger. Too loose (light leaks in round it): wrap a turn of tape round the inner tube.
- Wrinkled tracing paper: a wrinkly screen gives a wrinkly image. Tape it drum-tight.
- A torn or ragged pinhole. Push the pin straight through and turn it once; don't wiggle it. Make a new foil square if needed.
- A wrong distance mark. Check one camera: with the "30" line at the back edge of the outer tube, a ruler held along the outside from the pinhole should reach the screen end at 30 cm.

**First picture (5 min).** Take them outside. The image is best when the student is in shade and the scene is in sunlight. Make sure no one points at the Sun.

**Hole size (10 min). Expected results.**

| Hole | Diameter | Brightness (compared with pin) | Sharpness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pin | about 0.7 mm | 1× (dim) | sharp |
| Nail | about 3 mm | about $(3 \div 0.7)^2 \approx 18$× brighter | blurry |
| Pencil | about 7 mm | about $(7 \div 0.7)^2 = 100$× brighter | very blurry, just patches of colour |

Brightness goes with the **area** of the hole, so it goes with the diameter squared. Each point of the scene makes a spot on the screen about the size of the hole, so a 7 mm hole smears every detail across 7 mm, and a whole tree's image may only be 5 cm tall.

**Camera length (7 min).** A door 2 m tall, 10 m away: at 15 cm the image is $2 \times 0.15 \div 10 = 0.03$ m = 3 cm; at 30 cm it is 6 cm. Doubling $d_i$ doubles $h_i$. The image also gets dimmer, about a quarter as bright, because the same light is spread over four times the area. Measured heights usually agree with the doubling to within a few millimetres.

Where students get stuck: they move their own position between the two readings. They must stand still and only slide the tube.

### Step 4: How tall is it? (25 min)

**Theory (4 min).** Go through the rearrangement on the board with them. The step "flip both sides over" is new to some: $\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}$ means $\frac{b}{a} = \frac{d}{c}$, as long as nothing is zero. A safer route for those who prefer the golden rule: multiply both sides by $h_o$, then by $d_o$, then divide by $d_i$.

**Measuring (10 min).** Points to watch:

- $d_o$ is measured from the base of the object to where the student stands, along the ground.
- The camera should be held **level**. If they tilt it up to fit the top in, the screen is no longer parallel to the object and the answer comes out wrong. Step back instead.
- Read $h_i$ on the scale from the bottom of the image to the top. The edges are a little fuzzy; take the middle of the fuzz.
- Change everything to metres before calculating.

**Answer key for the worked example.** $h_o = 0.054 \times 20 \div 0.30 = 1.08 \div 0.30 = 3.6$ m.

**Typical answers.** A one-storey classroom block is about 3–4 m to the gutter; school flagpoles are usually 6–10 m; trees vary. Your own reference measurement is the best check. Good teams agree with it within about 10%.

**Second method (4 min).** A friend 1.60 m tall standing 20 m away with $d_i = 30$ cm gives an image $1.60 \times 0.30 \div 20 = 0.024$ m = 2.4 cm. If the tree's image is 5.4 cm, the tree is $1.60 \times 5.4 \div 2.4 = 3.6$ m. This method doesn't need $d_o$ or $d_i$ at all, so it checks those measurements.

**Explain to the guide (7 min).** Do the sign-off conversations while teams measure.

**The eye and phone cameras.** A lens collects light through a wide opening (so the image is bright) and bends all the rays from one point of the scene back to one point on the screen (so the image is sharp). In the eye the screen is the retina; in a phone it's the sensor. The image on your retina is upside down too; your brain sorts it out.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause and fix |
|---|---|
| No image at all | Pinhole blocked or not through the foil; scene too dark. Point at a sunlit scene from shade. |
| Image very faint, grey | Light leaking in: round the tubes, through thin card, or under the hood. Tape gaps, double the card, tuck the hood in. |
| Image blurry with the pin hole | Torn or oval hole, or a wrinkled screen. New foil, new hole. |
| Image doesn't fit on the screen | Too close. Step back, or slide the screen to 15 cm. |
| Two answers for the height disagree a lot | Usually $d_o$ measured to the wrong place, or the camera tilted. Measure again. |
| Dark room shows nothing | Too much stray light, or eyes not adjusted. Seal gaps; wait three minutes. |

---

## Judging the sign-off items

- **camera-works:** you look under the hood and see a recognisable, upside-down scene. It doesn't need to be sharp everywhere.
- **explains-image:** two straight rays, one from the top and one from the bottom of the object, crossing at the hole, and the words "light travels in straight lines". A diagram with rays bending at the hole doesn't pass.
- **hole-and-length:** two tables, and an explanation that a bigger hole lets in more light but each point becomes a bigger spot, so details overlap and blur. They should say a longer camera gives a bigger, dimmer image.
- **measures-height:** $h_i$, $d_i$, $d_o$ written with units, all converted to metres, the rearranged formula written before the numbers, and an answer within about 20% of your reference.
- **safety:** never pointed at the Sun, stood still under the hood.

## Notes for experts reviewing this draft

- The "best" pinhole diameter is often given as about $1.9\sqrt{d_i \lambda}$ (Rayleigh). For $d_i = 0.30$ m and $\lambda = 550$ nm this is about 0.8 mm, close to a sewing pin's hole. Holes much smaller than this get blurrier again because of diffraction; the draft doesn't teach this.
- The descriptions of image orientation (a top-to-bottom flip on the dark-room wall, a half-turn when viewed through the camera's screen) depend on which side of the screen the viewer looks from. Please check the wording is clear.
- The height method assumes the camera is held level and the object is vertical. Errors from tilting the camera, or from the camera being at eye height rather than ground level, have not been worked out here.
- Confirm that black plastic sheeting and A3 card are easy to find in Addis, and the curriculum tags (grade 10 light, grade 9 similar figures) against the current national curriculum.
