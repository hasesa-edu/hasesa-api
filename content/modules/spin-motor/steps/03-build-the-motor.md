## Do: wind the coil (8 minutes)

1. Take about 1 m of enamelled copper wire. Leave 5 cm straight at the start.
2. Wind 15 turns neatly round a AA battery or a marker pen. Slide the coil off.
3. Wrap each loose end two or three times round the coil, on **opposite sides**, to hold the turns together. Leave each end sticking straight out, about 4 cm long. These are the **legs**.
4. Check that the two legs are in one straight line through the middle of the coil. Hold a leg in each hand and roll the coil: it should turn like a wheel on an axle, not wobble.

## Do: scrape the legs (7 minutes)

The wire is covered with a thin, clear coat of enamel. It stops electricity. You must remove it where the legs touch the paper clips.

- **Leg 1:** sand the enamel off **all the way round**, from the tip to about 1 cm from the coil. Bare copper looks brighter and pinker.
- **Leg 2:** stand the coil upright on the table, like a wheel. Sand **only the top half** of this leg, over the same length. Leave the bottom half covered.

This half-and-half leg is the secret of the motor. Don't skip it.

## Do: the supports (8 minutes)

1. Unbend two large paper clips. Bend the top end of each into a small loop, just big enough for a leg to turn in freely.
2. Hold the other ends against the + and − ends of the D cell with a rubber band. Lay the cell on its side and fix it with modelling clay so it can't roll.
3. Put the magnet on top of the cell, in the middle.
4. Adjust the clips so the loops are level and the coil will spin about 5–10 mm above the magnet without touching it.

## Do: make it spin (15 minutes)

Rest the legs in the loops. Give the coil a gentle flick. It may need a few flicks in each direction.

If it rocks but doesn't spin, or doesn't move at all, check:

- **Contact.** Is leg 1 bare all the way round? Are the loops bare metal?
- **Balance.** Does the coil spin freely and smoothly when you turn it by hand? Straighten the legs.
- **Distance.** Is the coil close to the magnet, but not touching?
- **Heat.** Has it been running more than 10 seconds? Lift it off and let it cool.

When it spins, test your prediction from step 1: turn the battery round (or turn the magnet over). Which way does it spin now?

## Theory: why it keeps turning

Go back to "A magnet pushes on a current" in **Electromagnetism**. When current flows, the coil becomes a small electromagnet, and the magnet below pushes on it. But that push would only turn the coil to one position, then hold it there, like a compass needle settling north.

The half-scraped leg fixes this. For half of each turn, the bare side touches the clip and current flows, so the coil is pushed round. For the other half, the enamel side touches, the current stops, and the coil coasts round on its own speed until the current comes back on. A switch that turns the current on and off at the right moment in each turn is called a **commutator**. Every DC motor has one.

## Record
