## Theory: graphs

Read **Graphs and relationships**. You'll learn:

- which variable goes on which axis (time along the bottom, temperature up the side),
- how to choose a scale and label the axes with units,
- why you draw a smooth line of best fit instead of joining the dots,
- how the steepness (gradient) of a line shows a rate, here how fast the cup cools in °C per minute.

## Do: draw the cooling curves (10 minutes)

On one sheet of graph paper:

1. Time (min) on the x-axis, from 0 to 30. Temperature (°C) on the y-axis. It doesn't need to start at 0: from 30 °C to 65 °C fills the page better. Say so on the axis.
2. Plot the control cup with × and the insulated cup with ●.
3. Draw a smooth curve through each set and label it.

## Notice something puzzling

Look at the control cup's curve. It's not a straight line: it's **steeper at the start** and flatter later. The water lost more heat in the first 5 minutes than in the last 5.

Why? Heat flows faster when the difference in temperature is bigger. At the start, the water is about 40 °C hotter than the room; by the end, the difference is smaller, so the heat leaks out more slowly. A cup of hot water never cools **below** room temperature.

Work out the gradient of the control curve over the first 10 minutes, and over the last 10 minutes, in °C per minute. How do they compare?

## Compare the designs (5 minutes)

Every team writes its 30-minute drops on the board: control and insulated. Which design kept its heat best? Which material or which part of the design (the lid, the thickness, the foil) do you think made the biggest difference? Is that a fair conclusion, or did the teams change more than one thing?

## How a thermos works

A thermos flask is a bottle inside a bottle:

- **A vacuum** between the two walls. With no air there, heat can't cross by conduction or convection.
- **Silvered (shiny) walls** facing the gap. They give off and take in very little radiation.
- **A stopper** on top. It stops convection and evaporation out of the mouth, and it's made of plastic or cork, which conduct poorly.

The only easy way out left is along the thin neck, where the two walls meet. That's why a thermos still cools slowly, over many hours.
