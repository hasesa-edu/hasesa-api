## Do: three quick tests (20 minutes)

Your guide pours warm water (about 60 °C) into the cups for each test. Cups stay on the tray.

**Test A: spoons.** Stand a metal spoon, a wooden spoon and a plastic spoon in one cup of hot water. After 2 minutes, touch the top of each handle. Which is warmest? Rank them.

**Test B: lid or no lid.** Two cups with the same hot water. Put a piece of cardboard on one as a lid. After 3 minutes, lift the lid and look at its underside. Hold your hand a few centimetres above the open cup, then the same distance beside it. Where does it feel warmer?

**Test C: shiny or black.** Two cups with the same hot water. Wrap one in foil, shiny side out. Wrap the other in black paper. Hold your palm the same distance from the side of each, without touching. Then measure both temperatures now and again after 10 minutes (while you read the theory below).

Record what you feel and measure in a table.

## Notice something puzzling

The metal handle got warm, but the wooden one didn't, though both stood in the same water. The air above the open cup felt warmer than the air beside it. The underside of the lid was wet. And your palm felt more warmth from the black cup than from the shiny one, even though the water inside was the same temperature.

So heat must leave a cup in more than one way.

## Theory: heat transfer

Read **Heat transfer**. You'll learn:

- that heat always flows from hotter to colder,
- **conduction**: heat passed along through a solid, fast in metals, slow in wood, cloth and still air,
- **convection**: warm air or water rises and cooler fluid takes its place, and how a lid stops it,
- **radiation**: heat given off as infrared, strongly by black, dull surfaces and weakly by shiny ones,
- why materials that trap still air (wool, cotton, crumpled newspaper) are good insulators.

One more way, which you saw on the underside of the lid: **evaporation**. The fastest water particles escape from the surface as vapour, and they take energy with them. That's why blowing on hot tea cools it. From an open cup of hot water, evaporation carries away a big share of the heat. A lid stops it.
