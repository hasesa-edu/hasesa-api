# Keep it Hot: guide's notes

These notes are for the guide only. The module is one 2-hour session for up to 16 students in teams of three or four. It runs indoors on ordinary tables. You are the only person who handles hot water.

---

## Before the session

### 1. Shopping

- **Cups.** Identical cups, 2 per team plus 4 for the step 2 demonstrations and 2 for the hook. Clean food tins of about 400 mL work well; tape over any sharp rim. Thick plastic cups of one kind also work. Every cup in the class must be the same type, or teams can't compare results.
- **Thermometers.** Ideally 2 per team (one per cup), plus one for you. Digital probe thermometers are fastest to read. Red-liquid (alcohol) aquarium or kitchen thermometers are fine. **Never mercury.** Before the session, put all of them in the same cup of warm water and check they agree within about 1 °C; label any that read high or low.
- **Insulating materials.** A pile on a side table so teams can choose: newspaper, old cloth (a torn netela or a towel), cotton wool, dry straw, foil, cardboard for lids, rubber bands and string.
- **Hot water.** At least 3 L: one large thermos flask, or two. See below.

### 2. Pre-build

- **Lids.** Cut cardboard squares a little bigger than the cups, with a hole in the middle just big enough for a thermometer.
- **The hook set-up.** About 30–40 minutes before students arrive, fill a household thermos and one open cup with water at about 60 °C at the same time. Leave both on the table.
- **Hot water for the session.** Heat water at home or in the staff room and fill the flasks. In the room, check the temperature in a jug and add cold water until it reads about **60 °C** before pouring for students. Water in a good flask stays near its starting temperature for the whole session, so you may need to cool each batch.

---

## Safety briefing (read aloud in step 1)

- I pour all the hot water. It is never hotter than about 60 °C. Nobody touches a kettle.
- Cups stay on the trays on the table. Wrap your cup **before** I pour. You never lift or carry a cup of hot water.
- Spills get wiped at once.
- Digital or red-liquid thermometers only. If a glass one breaks, step back and call me.
- If hot water touches your skin, tell me straight away: we hold it under cool running water for 20 minutes.

Water at 60 °C can scald in a few seconds, so keep these rules strict even though it is "only warm water".

---

## Session plan

### Step 1: Cold buna (15 min)

Read the rules. Pour a little from the thermos and from the open cup into two clean cups and measure them. Expect the thermos to be at about 57–59 °C and the open cup at about 40–45 °C after 30–40 minutes, depending on the cup and the room.

Collect the 30-minute guesses. Most students guess too low (they expect the cup to be nearly cold). Say: "The thermos can't make heat. It can only stop heat leaving. Today you'll find every way heat leaves, and block them."

### Step 2: Where does the heat go? (35 min)

Pour about 150–200 mL of water at 60 °C for each test.

**Test A.** The metal handle warms within 1–2 minutes; the wood and plastic stay cool. An aluminium spoon shows it more strongly than a stainless-steel one (stainless steel is a weaker conductor than most metals, but still far better than wood). Answer: **conduction**; slowed by wood and plastic, which are insulators.

**Test B.** The underside of the lid is covered with droplets: water that evaporated and condensed. The air above the open cup feels warmer than beside it: warm, moist air rising. Answer: **convection and evaporation**; slowed by the lid.

**Test C.** Students should feel a little more warmth from the black cup. The temperatures after 10 minutes usually differ by only about 0.5–1.5 °C, with the shiny cup warmer. The difference is small because radiation is only one of the four routes, and most of the loss from an open cup goes out of the top by evaporation and convection. That is a good discussion point, not a failed test. Answer: **radiation**; reduced by shiny foil.

**Where students get stuck.** Many think cold "gets in" rather than heat getting out. Insist on "heat flows from hot to cold". Some think foil "keeps heat in" because it is metal; in fact foil is a good **conductor**, and it helps only by reflecting and not radiating. A tight foil wrap with no air layer does little for conduction.

### Step 3: Wrap a cup (45 min)

Keep the theory and plan to 10 minutes. Controlled variables to look for: the same type of cup, the same volume of water (200 mL), the same start temperature, the same place on the table, the same time between readings, the same thermometer method (stir, wait, read).

Pour each team's two cups one straight after the other, from the same flask. Start temperatures within about 2 °C of each other are fine; if they differ more, teams compare the **drop** rather than the final temperature. Pouring into a cold tin cools the water by only about 1–2 °C.

Teams then read both cups every 5 minutes. Rotate around the room and check that they stir, wait for the reading to settle, and put the lid back at once.

**Expected results** (200 mL, 60 °C start, room about 20 °C, tin cups). These are typical numbers; your room and cups will differ.

| Time (min) | Control (°C) | Well insulated, with lid (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 60.0 | 60.0 |
| 5 | 56.2 | 58.7 |
| 10 | 52.7 | 57.5 |
| 15 | 49.6 | 56.3 |
| 20 | 46.8 | 55.2 |
| 25 | 44.3 | 54.1 |
| 30 | 42.0 | 53.0 |

So the control drops about 18 °C and a good design about 7 °C. Most designs land somewhere between a 5 °C and a 12 °C drop. A cup with **only a lid** usually does surprisingly well, because the lid stops evaporation and convection from the top, the biggest losses from an open cup. That is a nice surprise to bring out in step 4.

### Step 4: The cooling curve (25 min)

**Worked gradients from the control column above:**

- first 10 minutes: $(60.0 - 52.7) \div 10 \approx 0.73$ °C/min,
- last 10 minutes: $(46.8 - 42.0) \div 10 = 0.48$ °C/min.

The control cools about one and a half times as fast at the start. Drops per 5 minutes fall from 3.8 °C to 2.3 °C. The reason: the rate of heat loss depends on the difference between the water and the room (about 40 °C at the start, about 22 °C at the end).

**Comparing designs.** When teams list their drops on the board, ask: "Can we say cotton is better than newspaper?" Usually not fairly, because teams changed several things at once (material, thickness, lid). A good answer says what single-variable test would settle it.

**Thermos.** If you have an old thermos, show the silvered inner bottle (don't break it: the vacuum flask is glass). Point out that the vacuum stops conduction and convection, the silvering cuts radiation, and the stopper stops convection and evaporation. The neck is the weak point.

Do the sign-off conversations while teams pour their water away and tidy up.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause and fix |
|---|---|
| Both cups cool at the same rate | Thermometer left in one cup changing its reading, or the lid left off. Check the method. Or the insulation is very thin: still a valid result. |
| Insulated cup cools faster than control | Wet wrapping (water spilled into it) cools by evaporation. Or the thermometers disagree: swap them and compare. |
| Readings jump around | Not stirring, or reading before the thermometer settles. Stir, wait 20–30 seconds, then read. |
| Start temperatures very different | Poured too far apart in time, or flask water too hot. Compare drops, not final temperatures. |
| Test C shows no difference | Normal over 10 minutes. Use the hand test and explain why radiation is a small share. |

---

## Judging the sign-off items

- **fair-test:** a written plan with at least four controlled variables, and the control cup really was identical and side by side.
- **readings:** 7 readings for each cup (0 to 30 minutes), with °C in the heading, none invented. Small gaps (a missed reading) are fine if marked.
- **graph:** time on the x-axis, temperature on the y-axis, units, a sensible scale, two labelled smooth curves (not dot-to-dot), and both 30-minute drops calculated correctly.
- **explains-routes:** names a route for each material that makes sense (newspaper, cloth, cotton, straw: conduction and convection, by trapping still air; foil: radiation; lid: convection and evaporation), and names the three parts of a thermos with what each blocks.
- **safety:** stayed at the tray, never lifted a hot cup, reported any spill.

- The *Heat transfer* concept unit doesn't mention evaporation; step 2 explains it in the text. Consider adding a short section to the unit.
