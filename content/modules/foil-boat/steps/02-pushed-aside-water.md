## Do: catch the pushed-aside water (15 minutes)

Work over a tray. You need a jug, a small catch bowl and the kitchen scale.

1. Weigh the empty catch bowl. Write its mass down.
2. Put a small plastic bottle with a little sand inside on the scale. Add or remove sand until it is about 100–200 g. Put the lid on tightly and write its mass down.
3. Stand the jug in the tray and fill it with water **right to the brim**, until a drop runs over. Wait until it stops dripping, then put the empty catch bowl under the spout or the edge.
4. Lower the bottle gently into the jug and let it float. Water spills over into the catch bowl.
5. When it stops dripping, weigh the catch bowl with the water in it. Subtract the empty bowl to get the mass of the spilled water.

Remember: 1 mL of water has a mass of 1 g. So the spilled water's mass in grams is also its volume in millilitres.

Try a second floating object too, for example a block of wood.

## Notice something puzzling

Compare the mass of the bottle with the mass of the water it pushed out. What do you notice? Is it a coincidence?

Now try the **stone**. Does it spill more or less water than its own mass? Why?

## Theory: density and forces

Read **Density**. You'll learn:

- that density is mass divided by volume, $\rho = m/V$, in g/cm³,
- that 1 mL is the same as 1 cm³, and water's density is about 1 g/cm³,
- that something denser than water sinks, and something less dense floats.

Then read the parts of **Forces and motion** on weight and on Newton's first law. You'll learn:

- that weight is a force, $W = mg$, measured in newtons,
- that when an object sits still, the forces on it are **balanced**.

Put them together. A floating object is not moving up or down, so the water must be pushing it **up** just as hard as gravity pulls it **down**. That upward push is called **upthrust** (or buoyancy). Your measurements show a rule that Archimedes found more than 2,000 years ago:

> **The upthrust on an object equals the weight of the water it pushes aside.**

A floating object sinks into the water until it has pushed aside its own weight of water. If it can't push aside that much, even when it is completely under, it sinks.

## Do it better: the ball and the boat (10 minutes)

Weigh a 20 cm × 20 cm sheet of foil. Then answer:

1. Aluminium has a density of 2.7 g/cm³. What is the volume of the foil when it is squeezed into a solid ball? ($V = m/\rho$.)
2. How much water can that ball push aside? Is that enough to hold up its weight?
3. Now fold it into a box shape 14 cm × 14 cm with walls 3 cm high. How much water could the box push aside before water pours over the top?

## Record

Write a table: object, its mass (g), mass of water pushed aside (g), floats or sinks. Under it, write the rule in your own words, and one sentence explaining why the ball sinks but the boat floats. Use the words **density** and **upthrust**.
