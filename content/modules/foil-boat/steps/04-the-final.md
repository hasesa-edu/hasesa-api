## Do: the final (10 minutes)

Each team brings its best boat to the guide's basin. One at a time, a member of **another** team adds the coins, one by one, spreading them evenly. The class counts out loud.

On the board, the guide records for every team: wall height, base size, predicted coins, actual coins.

## Notice something puzzling

Look at the board. Did the boat with the biggest volume carry the most? Were most predictions too high or too low? By about how much?

A boat almost never reaches its full prediction. Think about why: did water leak in at the corners, did the walls bend, did the coins pile up on one side and tip it?

## Back to the mystery (5 minutes)

Open the page titled *The boat mystery* from step 1. Next to each guess, write ✓ or ✗ and what you now know.

Answer the first question properly, in your own words: **why does the ball sink and the boat float?** Use these words: *density, volume, water pushed aside, upthrust, weight, balanced*.

## Real ships

- **Wide hulls.** A cargo ship is wide and flat-bottomed for the same reason as your boat: each extra centimetre it sinks pushes aside a huge amount of water, so it can take on a heavy load without sinking much deeper. The whole ship, steel plus the air inside, has a lower average density than water.
- **The load line.** On the side of a cargo ship you'll find a row of marks called the **Plimsoll line**. It shows how deep the ship may safely sit when loaded. Sea water is a little denser than fresh water (about 1.025 g/cm³ instead of 1.000), so a ship floats slightly higher in the sea than in a river. That's why there are separate marks for fresh water and for sea water.

## Explain it to the guide (10 minutes)

Your guide will ask each of you about your boat. Be ready to:

- show your displacement measurements from step 2 and say what they prove,
- show your prediction calculation, with units, and say why the real result was different,
- explain why the ball sinks and the boat floats,
- say whether a loaded ship sits higher or lower in the water when it moves from salty sea water into fresh water, and why.
