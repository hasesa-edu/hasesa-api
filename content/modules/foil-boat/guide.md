# Foil Boat: guide's notes

These notes are for the guide only. The module is one 2-hour session for up to 16 students in teams of three or four. It needs a room with tables you don't mind getting wet, and a tap or a bucket of water nearby.

---

## Before the session

### 1. Shopping

- **Foil.** One roll of ordinary kitchen foil is plenty. Cut it into **20 cm × 20 cm squares** with a ruler and scissors: 3 per team, plus 6 spares and 2 for your demonstration. Use the same roll for everyone, so every sheet has the same thickness and mass.
- **Coins.** About 100 identical 1-birr coins per team. A bank will give you coins for notes. Identical steel washers (one size, from a hardware shop in Merkato) work just as well. Don't mix coin types.
- **Basins.** One plastic basin per team, at least 25 cm across and 8 cm deep, plus one for the final. Half fill them before students arrive.
- **Scale.** At least one kitchen scale reading to 1 g. A 0.1 g scale (jewellery or pocket scale) is better for weighing the foil.
- **Displacement kit**, per team: a jug or tall container with a spout or a smooth rim, a small catch bowl, a tray. A small plastic bottle (250–330 mL) with a screw lid and a little sand. A stone about the size of an egg. A small block of wood.

### 2. Pre-build and pre-measure

- **Weigh 10 coins** and work out the mass of one coin. Write it on the board before the session. The numbers below use **7 g** as an example: replace them with your own.
- **Weigh one foil sheet.** Ordinary kitchen foil is about 0.015 mm thick, so a 20 cm square is about 1.5–2 g. Thicker "heavy-duty" foil is about 2.5 g.
- **Make your demonstration boat** from one sheet: base about 14 × 14 cm, walls 3 cm. Test it with coins so you know what it holds.
- **Make your foil ball** from another sheet and **test that it sinks**. A loosely crumpled ball traps air and floats. Squeeze it as hard as you can, then roll it on the table under your palm, or flatten it with a book or hammer. If it still floats, fold the sheet into a small square, press it flat and hard: a flat folded square with no air sinks reliably.

---

## Safety briefing (read aloud in step 1)

- Water stays in the basins and on the trays. Wipe spills straight away; tell me if the floor gets wet.
- Fold sharp foil edges over; don't run your fingers along a cut edge.
- Coins are counted out and counted back. Wash your hands after the session and before eating.

---

## Session plan

### Step 1: Steel that floats (15 min)

Read the safety points. Then drop in the bolt, then the foil ball, then float the boat and add coins one at a time with the class counting. Stop just before it sinks, or let it sink at the end: both work. Don't explain anything. Collect a few coin guesses on the board; most students guess far too low (5–20 coins).

Say: "Same metal, same mass, same water. Something about the **shape** changed. By the end you'll be able to predict how many coins a boat can carry before you load it."

### Step 2: Pushed-aside water (35 min)

**Overflow method.** The jug must be filled until it overflows and then left to stop dripping **before** the catch bowl goes under. Lower the bottle slowly; dropping it splashes water out and spoils the result.

**Expected results.** A 150 g bottle spills about 140–150 g of water. The result is usually a few grams **low**, because some water clings to the rim and spout. A wooden block of 60 g spills about 55–60 g. A stone of about 120 g spills about 45 g (stones are about 2.5–3 g/cm³): **much less** than its own mass, so the upthrust (about 0.45 N) can't hold up its weight (about 1.2 N), and it sinks.

The key idea to draw out: for every floating object, **mass of water pushed aside = mass of the object**. Draw the force diagram on the board: weight down, upthrust up, the same length, because the object is still (Newton's first law).

**"Do it better" answer key** (using a 1.6 g sheet):

1. $V = m/\rho = 1.6 \div 2.7 \approx 0.59$ cm³.
2. The ball can push aside at most 0.59 cm³ of water, which has a mass of 0.59 g. That's less than its own 1.6 g, so the upthrust is too small and it sinks. Same idea in density: the ball's density is 2.7 g/cm³, more than water's 1.0.
3. $14 \times 14 \times 3 = 588$ cm³, so it can push aside up to 588 g of water. The boat's **average** density (1.6 g of foil spread over 588 cm³ of space) is about 0.003 g/cm³, far less than water.

**Where students get stuck.** Many think heavy things sink and light things float. Ask: "Is a ship lighter than a bolt?" Others think the air inside the boat "lifts" it. The air helps only by taking up space without adding much mass; it is the water pushing up on the bottom that holds the boat.

### Step 3: Design your boat (45 min)

Keep the theory part short (5 minutes): read the pressure explanation together and check the worked example. The point is that "water pushed aside" and "pressure under the boat" are two ways of saying the same thing.

**Completed table:**

| Wall height $h$ (cm) | Base side (cm) | Volume (cm³) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 18 | 324 |
| 2 | 16 | 512 |
| 3 | 14 | 588 |
| 4 | 12 | 576 |
| 5 | 10 | 500 |

The best is between 3 and 4 cm. (The exact best, from calculus, is $h = 10/3 \approx 3.3$ cm, base 13.3 cm, volume about 593 cm³. Strong students can try $h = 3.5$: base 13 cm, volume 591.5 cm³.) Low wide boats and tall narrow boats both lose.

**Worked prediction** for a 14 × 14 × 3 cm boat of 1.6 g, with 7 g coins:

- maximum load $= 588 - 1.6 \approx 586$ g,
- maximum coins $= 586 \div 7 \approx 83$ coins.

**Expected results.** Well-made boats usually carry about 60–80% of the prediction: here, roughly 50–70 coins. Reasons: the walls lean outwards or sag, so the real volume is smaller than measured; water seeps in at the corners; coins piled on one side tip the boat and water pours over the low side; students stop when a little water comes in. Neat boats with sharp creases and evenly spread coins do best.

**Where students get stuck.** Cutting the corners instead of folding (that wastes foil and makes leaks: allow only folding). Forgetting to subtract the boat's mass (it barely matters here, which is itself worth pointing out). Measuring the outside instead of the inside, or the wall height along a sloping wall.

### Step 4: The final (25 min)

Run the final fast: one basin, teams in turn, another team loads the coins. Put the class results on the board as a table. Ask: "Who came closest to their prediction, as a percentage?" For an 83-coin prediction and a 62-coin result: $62 \div 83 \approx 75\%$.

**Ship question answer.** Fresh water is less dense than sea water, so the ship must push aside a **larger volume** of fresh water to get the same upthrust. It sits **lower** in fresh water. That's why the fresh-water load mark is higher up the hull than the sea-water mark.

Do the sign-off conversations while teams tidy up.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause and fix |
|---|---|
| The foil ball floats | Air trapped inside. Squeeze harder, roll it, or fold the sheet into a small flat square and press it hard. |
| Overflow result far too low | Jug not full to the brim at the start, or catch bowl moved. Refill until it drips, wait, and try again. |
| Overflow result too high | The object was dropped and splashed. Lower it gently. |
| Boat leaks at the corners | Corners cut or torn. Fold the corners flat against the walls; use a fresh sheet. |
| Boat tips over early | Coins piled on one side. Spread them evenly from the middle outwards. |
| Walls collapse | Creases not pressed hard. Run a fingernail or ruler along each fold. |

---

## Judging the sign-off items

- **displacement:** a table with the object's mass and the spilled water's mass, both in grams, and a sentence saying they are (nearly) equal for a floating object. Results within about 10% are fine.
- **prediction:** inside measurements, volume in cm³, the boat's mass subtracted, and the division by one coin's mass. Done **before** the test (check the order in the notebook).
- **boat-test:** a count of coins carried and the difference from the prediction, with at least one sensible reason for the difference.
- **explains-floating:** must link floating to **water pushed aside** (or upthrust equal to weight), not just "it's light" or "air holds it up". A strong answer mentions the average density of the boat with the space inside.
