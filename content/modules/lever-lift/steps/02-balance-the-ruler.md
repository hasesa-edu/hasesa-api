## Do: balance coins (15 minutes)

1. Lay a round pencil on the table. Rest the ruler across it at the **15 cm mark**, so it balances on its own. That point is the **pivot**.
2. Put **2 coins** on the 5 cm mark (10 cm from the pivot). Stack them so their middles are right on the mark.
3. Where must you put **2 coins** on the other side to balance? Try it.
4. Now put **4 coins** on the other side instead. Where do they balance?

Keep going with different numbers of coins on each side. Find at least six balances. Measure every distance **from the pivot** (the 15 cm mark), not from the end of the ruler.

| Left: coins | Left: distance from pivot (cm) | Right: coins | Right: distance from pivot (cm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 10 | 2 | |
| 2 | 10 | 4 | |

## Notice something puzzling

Four coins balanced two coins, even though four coins are twice as heavy. Something on the light side makes up for having less weight. What is it?

Look along each row of your table. Is there a calculation that gives the same answer for the left side and the right side?

## Theory: levers and moments

Read **Levers and moments**, the sections up to *The law of the lever*. You'll learn:

- the names **pivot**, **load** and **effort**,
- that the turning effect of a force, its **moment**, is force × distance from the pivot,
- the **law of the lever**: a lever balances when $F_1 \times d_1 = F_2 \times d_2$.

Here you can count coins instead of newtons, because every coin weighs the same.

## Do it better: predict, then test (10 minutes)

For each one, **write your prediction first**, then test it:

1. 3 coins at 8 cm on the left. Where must 2 coins go on the right?
2. 1 coin at 15 cm on the left. How many coins at 3 cm on the right?
3. 2 coins at 6 cm **and** 1 coin at 12 cm, both on the left. Where must 4 coins go on the right?

## Record

Add a column to your table: coins × distance, for each side. Write the law of the lever in your own words, in one sentence, using the words *pivot*, *distance* and *balance*.
