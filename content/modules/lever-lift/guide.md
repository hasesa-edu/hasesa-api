# Lever Lift: guide's notes

These notes are for the guide only. The module is one 2-hour session for up to 16 students in teams of three or four. Step 2 runs on desks; steps 1 and 3 need a hard, flat floor with about 3 × 2 m of clear space per plank (a corridor or the yard works).

The key design choice: the brick goes under the plank's **balance point** (close to its middle). Then the plank's own weight acts at the pivot and has no moment, so the simple law of the lever predicts the pull well. With the pivot near one end, a 5–6 kg plank adds a large moment of its own and the predictions fail badly. Keep this for strong students as an extension (see the end of step 3).

---

## Before the session

### 1. Shopping

- **Planks:** about 2.2 m × 20 cm × 3 cm, one per team plus one for the hook. Any sound timber works; reject planks with cracks, splits or knots wider than 2 cm. A plank like this weighs about 5–7 kg.
- **Pivots:** one fired brick per plank, and a 30 cm length of thick steel pipe (about 25 mm) or round pole. Tape the pipe across the top of the brick. The plank rocks on the pipe, which gives a clear pivot line.
- **Sand:** 15 kg per team, dry. Put it in a woven sack inside a second sack, squeeze the air out and tie it tightly, so it lies flat. Weigh each sack and write the mass on it. Wet sand is heavier: re-weigh if it has rained.
- **Luggage scales:** one per team. Test each with a known mass (a 10 kg bag of sugar or flour). If you can't get enough scales, use water bottles: a full 1 L bottle has a mass of about 1.03 kg, and a 0.5 L bottle about 0.52 kg. Plus a strong bag (a woven shopping bag) for each team.
- **For step 2:** 30 cm rulers, round pencils, 10 identical coins per team. 1-birr coins work well. Check every ruler balances near 15 cm.
- **For step 4:** scissors, a bottle opener, a hammer, a nail half driven into a block of wood, and a picture of a wheelbarrow.
- Rope, tape measures, markers, masking tape, gloves.

### 2. Set up the hook lever

Find the hook plank's balance point on the pipe and mark it. Mark 0.20 m and 0.50 m from the pivot on one side and 0.50 m and 1.00 m on the other. Tie a rope loop at 1.00 m. Lay the sack **across** the plank (so it doesn't spread far along it) with its middle at 0.20 m and tie it on with rope round the plank. Try the lift yourself: you should need a pull of about 3 kg.

---

## Safety briefing (read aloud in step 1)

- Never more than 15 kg of sand. The sack is always tied to the plank.
- Check the plank before every lift. A plank that creaks, splits or sags is retired.
- Feet out from under the plank and the sack. Nobody stands beyond either end of the plank.
- Lift slowly and lower slowly. If you let go of the rope, the sack drops and **your end of the plank flies up**. Keep your face away from the plank end above your hand.
- Nobody stands, sits or jumps on the plank, even "just to see".
- Moving the sacks: two people, or one person with bent knees and a straight back.

The real risks are a dropped sack on a foot and a plank end hitting a face when someone lets go. Watch for both.

## Session plan

### Step 1: Two fingers and a plank (15 min)

Read the rules first. Ask for a volunteer to try to lift the sack straight up with two fingers; stop them before they strain. Then set up the lever: sack middle at 0.20 m, pull at 1.00 m. Choose the smallest volunteer. The pull is about **3 kg** (about 30 N), easy with two fingers.

Then move the sack to 0.50 m: the pull rises to about **7.5 kg** and is noticeably harder. Then put the sack back at 0.20 m and pull at 0.50 m: about **6 kg**. Don't explain. Students should notice that the hand moves much further than the sack rises.

If the sack sits right over the brick, the plank just tips and the sack doesn't rise at all: it has no moment. Some students will guess "it gets easier".

### Step 2: Balance the ruler (35 min)

**Answers:**

- 2 coins at 10 cm balance 2 coins at **10 cm**, or 4 coins at **5 cm**.
- Prediction 1: 3 × 8 = 24, so 2 coins at **12 cm**.
- Prediction 2: 1 × 15 = 15, so **5 coins** at 3 cm.
- Prediction 3: 2 × 6 + 1 × 12 = 24, so 4 coins at **6 cm**. This one shows that moments on the same side **add**.

**Where students get stuck:**

- Measuring from the end of the ruler instead of the pivot. The table should have distances like 5, 10, 12, not 20, 25, 27.
- Coins not centred on the mark, or stacked crookedly. Balances within about half a centimetre are fine.
- A ruler that doesn't balance at 15 cm: use its real balance point as the pivot, and measure from there.
- Pencils that roll: tape the pencil to the desk.

The "puzzling" question should lead them to: on the light side the coins are further away. If no one spots coins × distance, ask them to multiply the two numbers on each side of one row.

### Step 3: Lift the sack (45 min)

Check every team's plank, sack knot and balance-point mark before they lift.

**Answer key** (15.0 kg sack, pull at 1.00 m):

| Sack's middle (m) | Working | Predicted pull | In newtons |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.20 | 15 × 0.20 ÷ 1.00 | 3.0 kg | 29 N |
| 0.30 | 15 × 0.30 ÷ 1.00 | 4.5 kg | 44 N |
| 0.50 | 15 × 0.50 ÷ 1.00 | 7.5 kg | 74 N |

Adjust for each team's sack mass. **Expected measurements** are 0–15% above the predictions: friction where the plank rocks on the pipe, a pull that isn't quite vertical, a balance-point mark a centimetre or two out, and reading the scale a moment late. A measurement **below** the prediction usually means the balance point was marked wrongly, or the sack has slipped closer to the pivot.

**The cost (sack at 0.30 m).** Hand down 10 cm, sack up about **3 cm**. Pull × distance: 4.5 kg × 0.10 m = 0.45; sack: 15 kg × 0.03 m = 0.45. In joules, 44 N × 0.10 m ≈ 4.4 J both ways. The work is the same: the lever trades distance for force. Measured values will be roughly equal, with the hand side a little bigger because of friction.

**Mechanical advantage** at 0.20 m is 15 ÷ 3 = 5; at 0.50 m it is 2.

**Where students get stuck:**

- Measuring the sack's distance to its edge instead of its middle.
- Pulling at an angle. The rope must hang straight down; an angled pull has a shorter perpendicular distance, so the scale reads more.
- Reading the scale while the sack is still on the floor. They read at the moment the sack's end lifts.

**Extension for fast teams.** Move the brick to 0.40 m from one end and repeat the 0.30 m lift. The prediction fails: the plank's own weight now has a moment. Weigh the plank on the luggage scale and include its moment (plank weight × distance from the pivot to the plank's middle) in the sum.

### Step 4: Levers everywhere (25 min)

**Answer key:**

| Lever | Pivot | Load | Effort | Class | Makes force bigger? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scissors | the screw | paper between the blades | fingers on the handles | first | yes, when you cut close to the screw |
| Bottle opener | the tip resting on top of the cap | the edge of the cap being lifted | hand on the handle | second | yes |
| Claw hammer pulling a nail | the hammer head on the wood | the nail in the claw | hand on the handle | first | yes |
| Wheelbarrow | the wheel's axle | the load in the tray | hands on the handles | second | yes |
| Forearm | the elbow | the bottle in the hand | the biceps, pulling about 4 cm from the elbow | third | no: it multiplies distance and speed |

For the forearm: the hand is about 35 cm from the elbow and the biceps pulls about 4 cm from it, so the biceps must pull roughly 9 times the weight of the bottle, but the hand moves 9 times as far and as fast. That's why we can throw.

Do the sign-off conversations in this step, team by team.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause and fix |
|---|---|
| Plank slides off the pipe | Floor not flat, or pipe not taped down. Put the brick on a flat spot, tape the pipe firmly, centre the plank. |
| Sack slides along the plank | Tie it with rope round the plank on both sides of the sack. |
| Readings jump around | Pull slowly and steadily; read the moment the sack's end lifts. Take the mean of three. |
| Measured pull far too small | Balance point marked wrongly, or the sack is closer to the pivot than measured. Re-find the balance point with the sack removed. |
| Measured pull far too big | Pull is at an angle, or the plank is rubbing on the brick, not resting on the pipe. |
| Luggage scale reads zero or drifts | Many digital scales must be switched on while hanging with nothing on them. Re-zero and test with a known mass. |

---

## Judging the sign-off items

- **balance:** at least four rows where coins × distance match on both sides (within about 5%), and one prediction written down **before** it was tested.
- **predicts-effort:** the working shows load × load distance = pull × pull distance, with distances measured from the pivot. For each distance, measured and predicted within 20%. If they are further out, accept a correct explanation of what went wrong plus a better repeat.
- **trade-off:** they measured both distances and can say the hand moves further by the same factor that the force is smaller.
- **classifies:** four levers correct, with pivot, load and effort marked. The bottle opener is the one most often wrong (called first class); ask them where it presses down.
- **safety:** if a student broke a plank rule after a reminder, discuss it with them before signing.

## Notes for experts reviewing this draft

- The 15 kg limit and the 2.2 m × 20 cm × 3 cm plank are chosen so the plank is far from its breaking load; please confirm for locally available timber (often eucalyptus or pine).
- When the plank tilts, the perpendicular distances of both vertical forces shrink by the same factor (cos θ), so the ratio, and the prediction, is unchanged. The tilt here is only about 5°.
- The plank rests on top of the pipe, so its centre of mass is slightly above the pivot line. This causes a very small extra moment when tilted and is ignored.
- Students may do the moment sums in kilograms (as kilogram-force) because g cancels. The theory reading and the table also ask for newtons, so the unit idea isn't lost. Check this is acceptable for the curriculum.
- Curriculum links (grade 9 force and motion, grade 10 work, energy and power) need confirming against the current national curriculum.
